\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Large language models (LLMs) increasingly rely on inference-time reasoning strategies that generate, revise, and aggregate multiple candidate solutions. Chain-of-thought prompting encourages models to expose intermediate reasoning \cite{wei2022cot}, self-consistency aggregates multiple independently sampled reasoning paths \cite{wang2023selfconsistency}, and iterative refinement methods allow a model to reconsider its own output through feedback \cite{madaan2023selfrefine,shinn2023reflexion}. Multi-agent debate (MAD) extends this idea from repeated reasoning within one model to interaction among several agents: each agent first proposes a solution, observes peer arguments, and revises its answer over multiple rounds \cite{du2023multiagent,liang2024debate,chen2024reconcile}. This paradigm is appealing because disagreement can expose mistakes that isolated reasoning would miss, while agreement among several agents appears to provide a natural signal of confidence.

Yet agreement is only useful when the evidence underlying it remains sufficiently independent. This distinction is easy to overlook in LLM debate. Unlike independent samples in classical ensemble reasoning, later-round votes are generated after agents have observed one another. An answer supported by three agents may therefore arise in fundamentally different ways: three agents may have reached it independently, or one agent may have proposed it and the others subsequently adopted it. These outcomes have the same vote count but different informational provenance. Social influence, sycophancy, and persuasion in interacting LLMs further complicate the interpretation of consensus \cite{zhang2024collaboration,sharma2024sycophancy,smit2024mad}. Consequently, the central reliability question is not merely \emph{how many agents agree}, but \emph{where that agreement came from}.

We begin by showing that this distinction matters because multi-agent debate is not information preserving. A correct hypothesis may appear during the interaction and nevertheless disappear before the final vote when its supporting agents revise toward an incorrect alternative. We refer to this phenomenon as \emph{correct-hypothesis extinction}. Across our core experiments, preserving the complete hypothesis history raises candidate recall from approximately $0.64$ to $0.76$, revealing substantial information that a conventional final-round decision discards. More broadly, the pre-debate independent decision outperforms the debated final majority on four of five core benchmarks, showing that additional interaction is not uniformly constructive. These observations motivate a shift from treating debate solely as an answer-improvement process to treating it as a dynamic information system in which useful hypotheses can be created, propagated, and destroyed.

A natural response is to predict which debates are about to fail and intervene selectively. We find, however, that detecting failure and predicting the value of intervention are fundamentally different problems. Extinction itself is predictable: a hazard model achieves an ROC--AUC of approximately $0.76$, and an oracle intervention analysis exposes a large potential improvement of $16.5$ percentage points. Nevertheless, across five successive attempts with pre-specified acceptance gates, using increasingly rich pre-intervention signals, we cannot reliably identify which individual debates will actually benefit from intervention. Cheap structural features, semantic representations, active self-diagnosis, placebo-controlled response probes, and mid-debate trajectories all fail the same benefit-prediction gate. Thus, \emph{failure predictability does not imply intervention actionability}. Rather than continuing to search for a controller that targets individual debates, we address the information loss structurally.

Our resulting view is \emph{hypothesis preserving}. Instead of allowing an agent's latest answer to overwrite earlier candidates, we retain the set of hypotheses that appeared throughout the frozen debate history and attach a provenance record to each candidate. This record captures how the hypothesis entered and survived the interaction, including its number of independent round-$0$ origins, cumulative support, persistence, and subsequent social adoption. The resulting representation separates two quantities that ordinary majority voting conflates: the \emph{magnitude} of consensus and the \emph{origin} of consensus. The analysis is performed post hoc over frozen debate transcripts and requires no additional LLM calls; an end-to-end live non-destructive debate protocol is left for future work.

This provenance view reveals the central empirical finding of the paper: \emph{not all consensus is equally informative}. First, hypothesis provenance is strongly associated with correctness across model families, task families, model scales, and agent counts. More importantly, this relationship cannot be explained merely by total support. The number of independent origins and total support are strongly correlated ($r\approx0.85$), so we explicitly compare hypotheses at matched levels of total consensus. Holding total support fixed, an additional independent round-$0$ supporter remains strongly associated with correctness, with a partial log-odds coefficient of $\beta_{\mathrm{ind}}=0.589$ and a clustered-bootstrap $95\%$ confidence interval of $[0.512,0.671]$. A clean decomposition that separates genuinely independent origins from true social-adoption events makes the asymmetry concrete: the per-origin log-odds coefficient ($0.92$) far exceeds that of genuine peer-adoption ($-0.04$, not significant), a gap of $\Delta\beta=0.96$ with a $95\%$ confidence interval of $[0.88,1.05]$. Crucially, this is not an artifact of easy questions: a within-question conditional logistic regression that absorbs each question's fixed effect still yields a positive independent-origin effect ($\beta_{\mathrm{ind}}=0.47$, $[0.42,0.53]$), and within the same question the correct hypothesis carries more independent origins in $75\%$ of correct/incorrect pairs. The effect remains significant on seven of eight benchmarks and all five ensemble configurations, spanning five open-weight model families from roughly $3$B to $14$B parameters and agent populations of $N\in\{3,5\}$. StrategyQA is the principal boundary case.

A matched-support comparison makes the phenomenon visible without any model: within each fixed-support stratum, empirical correctness rises monotonically with the number of independent origins (Figure~\ref{fig:matched}). Importantly, we interpret this as an \emph{epistemic signal}, not a causal effect: independently generated agreement is associated with correctness after controlling for consensus, but the observational logs do not establish that increasing independent support would itself make an answer correct.

This conditional relationship also clarifies why provenance-aware reranking produces real but non-universal decision gains. Total support already captures much of the same information as independent support; adding provenance origin to raw support improves candidate-level AUC by only approximately $0.007$. Thus, a strong conditional relationship need not translate into a large incremental ranking advantage. Consistent with this explanation, a frozen provenance-aware selector improves over the deployed final majority in regimes where two conditions are simultaneously satisfied: provenance must discriminate correct from incorrect hypotheses, and a recoverable correct hypothesis must be available in the preserved pool. Under these conditions the selector improves pooled accuracy from $0.528$ to $0.564$ (paired $\Delta=+0.035$, $95\%$ CI $[+0.022,+0.049]$) with no additional LLM calls and also outperforms the influence-based voting rule inherited from our earlier work. Outside these regimes, the gain is statistically indistinguishable from zero rather than universally positive. Indeed, the selector barely exceeds a strikingly cheap baseline: taking the pre-debate (round-$0$) independent majority already reaches $0.560$ using only the first of roughly three debate rounds --- about $3$ LLM calls versus $\approx\!8.7$ for the full debate. The most compute-efficient reliable rule is therefore to \emph{trust independent answers rather than debate them}: a compute-matched self-consistency control confirms the direction, since at the debate's inference budget repeated independent sampling of the same models outperforms the debated majority ($0.610$ vs.\ $0.530$; Appendix~\ref{app:e26}). We do not present this as a baseline that defeats our method, nor do we propose provenance-aware selection as a replacement for self-consistency as a deployment method; rather, the self-consistency result is \emph{independent confirmatory evidence} for our mechanism --- keeping samples independent beats letting them become socially coupled --- and provenance is what \emph{explains why}, and identifies what information conventional debate aggregation destroys. We accordingly treat provenance-aware selection as a downstream application of the finding, not as the paper's central claim.

These results also refine an influence-centered view of multi-agent debate. Existing work has investigated communication topology, agent collaboration, persuasion, sycophancy, and which agents exert disproportionate influence over a group \cite{li2024sparse,zhang2024collaboration,sharma2024sycophancy}. Influence is important for describing how opinions propagate, but it does not determine whether the propagated hypothesis is correct. Our experiments show that an influence-aware voting rule can underperform plain majority voting, whereas hypothesis-level provenance remains strongly associated with correctness. The relevant unit of analysis is therefore not only the \emph{agent} that leads the debate, but the \emph{evidential history} of the hypothesis being selected.

The contributions of this work are fivefold:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Correct-hypothesis extinction.} We identify and quantify a failure mode in which a correct hypothesis is generated during debate but disappears before the final decision, exposing substantial recoverable information that conventional final-round aggregation discards.
    \item \textbf{Failure predictability is not intervention-benefit predictability.} We show that extinction can be detected and has a large oracle intervention ceiling, yet five increasingly informative benefit-prediction attempts with pre-specified gates fail to identify which individual debates should be targeted, motivating a structural rather than selective remedy.
    \item \textbf{Hypothesis preservation and provenance.} We formulate a non-destructive representation of frozen debate histories in which previously generated hypotheses are retained and annotated by their origin, support, persistence, and adoption history, enabling reliability analysis without additional LLM inference.
    \item \textbf{Consensus is not epistemically homogeneous.} At matched total support, independently generated agreement carries substantially more correctness signal than socially adopted agreement, and the effect survives a within-question control that absorbs each question's fixed effect. This relationship is robust across seven of eight benchmarks and all five ensemble configurations, including a $14$B anchor and $N=5$ debates.
    \item \textbf{From epistemic signal to conditional decision utility.} We show that provenance-aware reranking can convert part of the preserved headroom into accuracy gains with no additional LLM calls, while identifying the conditions under which such gains are possible and explaining why they are necessarily regime dependent. We position this as a downstream \emph{application} that explains and partially exploits the finding, not as a deployment method competitive with self-consistency; the paper's contribution hierarchy is the scientific finding first, the preservation-and-provenance framework second, and the selector as an application third.
\end{itemize}

Taken together, our results suggest a different principle for reliable collective reasoning with LLMs: consensus should not be treated as a homogeneous count of votes. Reliability depends not only on how much agreement exists, but also on how that agreement was formed. Preserving candidate hypotheses and tracking their epistemic provenance provides a way to distinguish independently corroborated evidence from socially adopted consensus, moving the analysis of multi-agent reasoning beyond influence and toward the origin of collective belief.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

\subsection{Inference-Time Reasoning and Self-Refinement}
\label{subsec:rw_reasoning}

A broad family of inference-time methods improves LLM reasoning by generating additional intermediate computation rather than changing model parameters. Chain-of-thought prompting elicits explicit reasoning trajectories \cite{wei2022cot}, while self-consistency samples multiple trajectories and selects an answer through agreement across independently generated chains \cite{wang2023selfconsistency}. Self-Refine repeatedly revises an answer using model-generated feedback \cite{madaan2023selfrefine}, and Reflexion equips language agents with verbal feedback that can influence subsequent attempts \cite{shinn2023reflexion}. These approaches establish that repeated inference can expose and correct errors, but they differ from multi-agent debate in an important respect: agreement obtained from independently sampled trajectories has a clearer interpretation than agreement obtained after agents have observed and influenced one another. Self-consistency establishes the value of \emph{aggregating independent samples}. Our question is different: once agents \emph{interact}, does an equal amount of observed consensus retain equal evidential value? We show that it does not --- at matched support magnitude, reliability depends systematically on whether agreement was independently generated or socially acquired. This is precisely why our independent-origin signal is not self-consistency under another name: self-consistency asks whether adding independent samples improves selection, whereas we hold the observed consensus \emph{fixed} inside an interactive debate and vary only its provenance. Our compute-matched self-consistency control (Appendix~\ref{app:e26}) is, in this light, independent confirmatory evidence for the same mechanism --- at equal inference budget, keeping samples independent outperforms letting them become socially coupled through debate.

\subsection{Multi-Agent Debate and Collaborative LLM Reasoning}
\label{subsec:rw_mad}

Multi-agent debate has emerged as a prominent strategy for collective LLM reasoning. Du et al. demonstrate that iterative exchange among multiple language-model instances can improve factuality and reasoning \cite{du2023multiagent}. Subsequent work explores divergent debate to increase reasoning diversity \cite{liang2024debate}, round-table consensus across heterogeneous models \cite{chen2024reconcile}, sparse communication topologies that reduce interaction cost \cite{li2024sparse}, and broader design principles and interventions for multi-LLM debate \cite{estornell2024multillm}. General multi-agent frameworks such as CAMEL and AutoGen further illustrate the growing role of structured LLM-to-LLM interaction in reasoning and task execution \cite{li2023camel,wu2023autogen}.

Most of this literature evaluates debate through final-task accuracy, consensus, communication efficiency, or architectural choices. Such metrics implicitly treat the final state as the primary object of interest. Our work instead analyzes the \emph{trajectory of hypotheses} through the interaction. This exposes correct-hypothesis extinction: information can be present during debate and absent at the final vote. The distinction motivates hypothesis preservation and changes the relevant question from whether interaction reaches consensus to whether the system retains and correctly interprets the evidence that generated that consensus.

\subsection{Social Influence, Persuasion, and Sycophancy in LLM Agents}
\label{subsec:rw_social}

LLM agents do not exchange information neutrally. Social-psychological analyses show that collaboration patterns, agent roles, and interaction structures affect collective behavior \cite{zhang2024collaboration}. Sycophancy studies demonstrate that language models may align their responses with user or contextual preferences even when such agreement is not epistemically justified \cite{sharma2024sycophancy}. Evaluations of multi-agent debate similarly report that additional discussion is not uniformly beneficial and that debate strategy can substantially alter final performance \cite{smit2024mad}. More recent work has explicitly examined sycophancy mitigation in multi-agent consensus, reinforcing the concern that apparent agreement may partly reflect interaction dynamics rather than independent corroboration \cite{zhang2025consensagent}.

These studies motivate analyzing \emph{how} beliefs propagate, but influence and correctness are distinct. Agent-level centrality can identify who is followed, just as PageRank-like scores quantify structural importance in a directed interaction graph \cite{page1998pagerank}, yet a highly influential agent can propagate either a correct or an incorrect hypothesis. Our prior conference work used this agent-centric perspective to study who leads a debate; the present work shifts the unit of analysis from agents to hypotheses. We record whether support was independently generated or socially adopted and show that, even at equal total support, these forms of agreement carry different correctness signal. This establishes epistemic provenance as complementary to, and more directly relevant for reliability than, influence alone.

\subsection{Consensus, Independent Evidence, and the Wisdom of Crowds}
\label{subsec:rw_crowds}

The interpretation of consensus has long depended on assumptions about independence, from Condorcet's jury theorem \cite{condorcet1785essai} and Galton's ox-weighing experiment \cite{galton1907vox} to modern treatments of collective judgement \cite{surowiecki2004wisdom}. Classical wisdom-of-crowds reasoning is strongest when individual judgments contain sufficiently independent information, so that errors can cancel rather than reinforce one another; a classic result is that social influence can \emph{undermine} the wisdom-of-crowds effect by narrowing the diversity of estimates \cite{lorenz2011crowd}, and information cascades show how sequential observation of others can drive a group toward a shared, possibly wrong, position \cite{bikhchandani1992cascades}. The same intuition is formalized in ensemble learning, where classifier \emph{diversity} measures (e.g.\ disagreement and the $Q$-statistic) relate ensemble accuracy to how uncorrelated the members' errors are \cite{kuncheva2003diversity}. Our independent-versus-adopted decomposition can be read as a debate-time, per-hypothesis analogue of this diversity principle: it asks not how diverse the agents are in aggregate, but how much of a specific hypothesis's support was produced before the agents' errors could become correlated through interaction. Crucially, the effect of influence is not uniformly harmful: appropriately structured social exchange can also improve collective estimates \cite{becker2017crowd}, so the relevant question is not whether agents interact but whether the resulting agreement retains independent information. Recent behavioral work likewise shows that convergence among independent and approximately unbiased informants can rationally signal competence and accuracy, while systematic shared biases weaken this inference \cite{pfander2025crowd}. This perspective is highly relevant to multi-agent LLM systems because later-round answers are explicitly conditioned on peer responses and therefore cannot be interpreted as independent observations.

Our results provide an LLM-specific operationalization of this principle. Rather than treating independence as an unobserved assumption, we track it directly through hypothesis provenance. We distinguish round-$0$ independent origins from support acquired after social exposure and compare them while holding total support fixed. The resulting difference is substantial: independently generated support remains strongly associated with correctness after controlling for raw consensus. In this sense, our work connects multi-agent LLM debate to the broader principle that the reliability of collective agreement depends on the dependence structure of the evidence producing it.

\subsection{Answer Selection, LLM-as-a-Judge, and Reliability Aggregation}
\label{subsec:rw_selection}

A separate line of work studies how to select among multiple model outputs. Self-consistency uses answer frequency over sampled reasoning paths \cite{wang2023selfconsistency}, while heterogeneous-agent systems frequently use majority voting, confidence weighting, or consensus-based aggregation \cite{chen2024reconcile,estornell2024multillm}. LLM-as-a-Judge methods instead ask a capable model to evaluate candidate responses directly; MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena helped establish the usefulness, as well as the biases and limitations, of this paradigm \cite{zheng2023judging}. In multi-agent settings, however, an external judge introduces additional inference cost and may solve the underlying problem more effectively than it discriminates among imperfect candidates.

Our approach differs from both answer-frequency aggregation and external judging. We exploit metadata already contained in the debate trajectory and therefore add no new LLM calls. More importantly, the provenance analysis is not introduced merely as a new scoring heuristic. Its primary role is explanatory: it reveals that equal consensus counts can encode different amounts of epistemic evidence. The resulting selector is intentionally treated as a downstream application, and we characterize rather than hide its applicability boundary. This separates the near-universal empirical regularity---independent provenance carries more correctness signal---from the more conditional question of whether that signal yields additional end-task accuracy over a strong consensus baseline.

\subsection{Positioning of This Work}
\label{subsec:rw_positioning}

The above literature reveals three unresolved gaps. First, existing MAD evaluations largely focus on final answers and therefore do not explicitly quantify correct hypotheses that appear and later disappear. Second, work on collaboration and influence explains how agents affect one another but does not determine whether socially propagated agreement should carry the same epistemic weight as independently generated agreement. Third, aggregation methods usually operate on the magnitude of support or on an external evaluator, without explicitly modeling the origin of that support. We address these gaps through a unified trajectory-level analysis: we quantify correct-hypothesis extinction, preserve the full candidate history, attach provenance to each hypothesis, and test whether the origin of agreement remains informative after controlling for total consensus. The resulting finding---that independently generated consensus carries substantially more correctness signal than socially adopted consensus at matched support, even within a question---is the central distinction between our approach and prior influence-, consensus-, or judge-based methods.


\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem_formulation}

This section fixes notation and defines the two objects the paper studies: the \emph{extinction} of a
correct hypothesis during debate, and the \emph{epistemic provenance} of the agreement that supports a
hypothesis. Throughout, we separate the deployed debate protocol, which generates the transcripts, from
our post-hoc analysis, which reads them; the analysis introduces no new model calls. The complete
experimental protocol is deferred to Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}.

\subsection{Multi-Agent Debate as Sequential Belief Revision}
\label{subsec:mad_formulation}

Let $\mathcal{A}=\{a_1,\dots,a_N\}$ be a set of $N$ heterogeneous LLM agents. Given a question $q$ drawn
from a benchmark $\mathcal{D}$ with gold answer $y^\star(q)$, the agents debate for at most $R=3$ synchronous
rounds, indexed $r\in\{0,1,2\}$. We write $x_i^{(r)}$ for the answer proposed by agent $a_i$ in round $r$, and
$\bar{x}=\mathrm{norm}(x)$ for a normalized answer, so that surface variants of the same answer are
treated as identical. At round $0$ each agent answers \emph{independently},
$x_i^{(0)}=a_i(q)$, with no knowledge of its peers. In every subsequent round the agents revise
\emph{simultaneously}, each conditioning on the previous round's answers of all peers:
\begin{equation}
x_i^{(r)} \;=\; a_i\!\Big(q,\; x_i^{(r-1)},\; \{x_k^{(r-1)}\}_{k\ne i}\Big),\qquad r\ge 1 .
\end{equation}
The debate stops early upon consensus, which is checked \emph{after each revision round} (i.e.\ from round
$r\ge 1$); otherwise it runs to the round cap. We write $T\le R-1=2$ for the index of a debate's final round.
Because the consensus check begins at round $1$, every debate performs at least one revision round, so a
hypothesis on which all $N$ agents already agree at round $0$ ($n_{\mathrm{ind}}=N$) still accrues further
cumulative support in round $1$; this is why $n_{\mathrm{sup}}$ can exceed $n_{\mathrm{ind}}$ even at full
independent consensus (e.g.\ the $n_{\mathrm{sup}}{=}6,\,n_{\mathrm{ind}}{=}3$ stratum in
Table~\ref{tab:matched}). The deployed system returns the majority answer over the agents' final responses,
$\hat{y}_{\mathrm{maj}}(q)=\arg\max_{c}\sum_{i}\mathbb{1}[\bar{x}_i^{(T)}=c]$, with ties broken
deterministically. This synchronous, peer-symmetric protocol is what makes belief revision --- and hence
provenance --- attributable: a change at round $r$ can be ascribed to what an agent saw at round $r-1$.

\subsection{Hypotheses and Debate Trajectories}
\label{subsec:hypothesis_trajectories}

We treat each distinct normalized answer value that appears in a debate as a \emph{hypothesis} $h$. Its
round-$r$ supporter set and the round-$r$ hypothesis set are
\begin{equation}
S^{(r)}(h)=\{\,i : \bar{x}_i^{(r)}=h\,\},\qquad
H^{(r)}=\{\,\bar{x}_i^{(r)} : i=1,\dots,N\,\}.
\end{equation}
A vanilla debate reports only the \emph{final pool} $H^{\mathrm{fin}}=H^{(T)}$. We contrast it with the
\emph{history pool} $H^{\mathrm{hist}}=\bigcup_{r\le T}H^{(r)}$, the set of all hypotheses that ever
appeared. The two coincide only if no hypothesis is ever abandoned; in general
$H^{\mathrm{fin}}\subseteq H^{\mathrm{hist}}$, and the difference is exactly the information a final-round
decision discards. We distinguish two notions of consensus magnitude: the \emph{final-round vote count}
$n_{\mathrm{final}}(h)=\lvert S^{(T)}(h)\rvert$, which the deployed majority actually uses, and the
trajectory-level \emph{cumulative support} $n_{\mathrm{sup}}(h)=\sum_{r=0}^{T}\lvert S^{(r)}(h)\rvert$,
which counts an answer's total presence over the debate; our matched-consensus analysis holds each fixed in
turn (Section~\ref{subsec:not_all_consensus_equal}). The \emph{persistence} of a hypothesis is the number of
rounds in which it is alive, $n_{\mathrm{per}}(h)=\lvert\{r : S^{(r)}(h)\ne\varnothing\}\rvert$.

\subsection{Correct-Hypothesis Extinction}
\label{subsec:extinction_definition}

A debate on question $q$ exhibits a \emph{correct-hypothesis extinction event} when the gold answer lives in
the history pool but not in the final-round pool:
\begin{equation}
E(q)=\mathbb{1}\!\big[\,y^\star(q)\in H^{\mathrm{hist}}(q)\ \wedge\ y^\star(q)\notin H^{\mathrm{fin}}(q)\,\big].
\label{eq:extinction_event}
\end{equation}
To quantify how often extinction destroys \emph{correct} information we measure the probability that the gold
answer survives to the final round versus anywhere in the history,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{CR}_{\mathrm{fin}}=\Pr\!\big(y^\star\in H^{\mathrm{fin}}\big),\quad
\mathrm{CR}_{\mathrm{hist}}=\Pr\!\big(y^\star\in H^{\mathrm{hist}}\big),\quad
\Delta\mathrm{CR}=\mathrm{CR}_{\mathrm{hist}}-\mathrm{CR}_{\mathrm{fin}} .
\label{eq:extinction}
\end{equation}
Since $H^{\mathrm{fin}}\subseteq H^{\mathrm{hist}}$, the two views coincide: the \emph{extinction rate} is
exactly $\mathrm{ER}=\tfrac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{q}E(q)=\Delta\mathrm{CR}$. This quantity is the maximum gain
attributable \emph{specifically} to recovering hypotheses lost before the final round, relative to a perfect
selector restricted to the final-round pool; the additional gain of a perfect final-round selector over the
deployed majority is a separate term (Section~\ref{par:preservation_headroom}).

\subsection{Epistemic Provenance of Agreement}
\label{subsec:provenance_definition}

Two hypotheses can carry identical total support yet have been produced in fundamentally different ways.
We decompose the support of $h$ by \emph{origin}. Its \emph{independent support} is the number of agents
that proposed it before observing any peer,
\begin{equation}
n_{\mathrm{ind}}(h)=\lvert S^{(0)}(h)\rvert ,
\end{equation}
where ``independent'' means \emph{interaction-independent} --- produced before any peer exposure --- and
\emph{not} statistically independent, since the agents share overlapping pretraining. Its
\emph{socially adopted support} counts genuine adoption events: agent $i$ switches onto $h$ at round
$r\ge 1$, did not hold it at $r-1$, and \emph{a peer was offering it} at $r-1$,
\begin{equation}
A_i^{(r)}(h)=\mathbb{1}\!\big[\,\bar{x}_i^{(r)}=h\ \wedge\ \bar{x}_i^{(r-1)}\ne h\ \wedge\ \exists\,j\ne i:\ \bar{x}_j^{(r-1)}=h\,\big],
\qquad
n_{\mathrm{adopt}}(h)=\sum_{r=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{N} A_i^{(r)}(h) .
\label{eq:adopt}
\end{equation}
The peer condition is essential: without it, an agent that independently generates a \emph{new} answer
mid-debate would be miscounted as ``adopting'' it. We also deliberately avoid the residual
$n_{\mathrm{sup}}-n_{\mathrm{ind}}$ as ``inherited support'', since it absorbs an originating agent's own
persistence. Collecting the pieces --- with $r_{\mathrm{first}}(h)=\min\{r:S^{(r)}(h)\ne\varnothing\}$ ---
each hypothesis is summarized by a \emph{provenance vector}
\begin{equation}
\phi(h)=\big[\,n_{\mathrm{sup}}(h),\ n_{\mathrm{ind}}(h),\ n_{\mathrm{adopt}}(h),\ n_{\mathrm{per}}(h),\ r_{\mathrm{first}}(h)\,\big]^\top .
\label{eq:provvec}
\end{equation}
Majority voting observes only the final-round count $n_{\mathrm{final}}$ and ignores the origin-aware
decomposition of support; our question is whether the finer $\phi(h)$ carries additional correctness signal.

\subsection{Research Hypotheses}
\label{subsec:research_hypotheses}

The paper is organized around three headline hypotheses of increasing sharpness:
\begin{itemize}
    \item[\textbf{H1}] \textbf{(Extinction).} Debate destroys correct answers it once held:
    $\Delta\mathrm{CR}>0$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:extinction}). As a supporting behavioral observation, the pre-debate
    independent decision is often no worse than the debated final decision.
    \item[\textbf{H2}] \textbf{(Provenance predicts correctness).} A hypothesis's own provenance carries
    predictive information about its correctness beyond chance --- in particular independent origin
    ($n_{\mathrm{ind}}$) and persistence ($n_{\mathrm{per}}$), whose reliability curves are (overall)
    monotone increasing.
    \item[\textbf{H3}] \textbf{(Origin beyond magnitude).} Holding consensus magnitude fixed, independent
    origins carry additional correctness signal: the partial effect of $n_{\mathrm{ind}}$ given
    $n_{\mathrm{sup}}$ is strictly positive ($\beta_{\mathrm{ind}}>0$). Moreover, in a decomposition
    separating independent origins, peer-compatible adoption events, and persistence, the independent-origin
    coefficient exceeds the adoption coefficient ($\beta_{\mathrm{ind}}-\beta_{\mathrm{adopt}}>0$).
\end{itemize}
The clause ``at matched consensus'' in H3 is load-bearing. Independent support and total support are
strongly collinear (Pearson $r\approx0.83$), so H2 alone cannot separate \emph{how much} agreement exists
from \emph{how it was formed}; H3 sharpens H2 by isolating the origin signal from consensus magnitude, and
is an empirical, observational relationship rather than a demonstrated causal mechanism.


\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Framework Overview}
\label{subsec:framework_overview}

Our method is a three-stage analysis of frozen debate transcripts --- \emph{preserve}, \emph{track
provenance}, and \emph{select} --- summarized in Figure~\ref{fig:framework}. We (i) retain every
hypothesis that appears in the debate rather than only the final round; (ii) annotate each retained
hypothesis with a provenance record describing how its support was formed; and (iii) optionally re-select
the final answer from the preserved pool using that record. All three stages operate post hoc on stored
transcripts and add \emph{no} LLM inference; the selection stage is therefore a drop-in replacement for
majority voting with no additional LLM inference. We stress that the analysis is offline: we do not run a live
non-destructive debate, and we quantify what preservation and provenance \emph{could} recover from
transcripts a standard debate already produces.

\subsection{Preserving the Hypothesis Space}
\label{subsec:hypothesis_preservation}

\paragraph{History-preserving candidate pool.}
\label{par:history_pool}
The core structural change is to accumulate hypotheses into a running pool
$\mathcal{H}_t=\bigcup_{r=0}^{t}H^{(r)}$ through a \emph{preservation operator}
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{H}_t=\mathcal{P}\big(\mathcal{H}_{t-1},H^{(t)}\big)=\mathcal{H}_{t-1}\cup H^{(t)},
\qquad \mathcal{H}_{-1}=\varnothing,
\label{eq:preserve_op}
\end{equation}
and to draw the final answer from $\mathcal{H}_T=H^{\mathrm{hist}}$ rather than from $H^{\mathrm{fin}}=H^{(T)}$.
Because a hypothesis, once generated, is never removed, the candidate space obeys a \emph{monotone
preservation invariant}
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;H^{(t)}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_t\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{t+1}\quad\text{for all }t\;}
\label{eq:preserve_inv}
\end{equation}
--- a property a destructive final-round vote lacks. Intuitively, debate may freely revise beliefs, but the
system-level candidate space never forgets a generated hypothesis. This directly counters both extinction
mechanisms (Section~\ref{par:extinction_anatomy}): a correct hypothesis abandoned through social adoption or
self-error drift remains recoverable.

\paragraph{Reconstruction from frozen debate trajectories.}
\label{par:posthoc_reconstruction}
We reconstruct $H^{\mathrm{hist}}$ from stored transcripts using a robust answer extractor that recovers
answers hidden by response truncation (e.g.\ a \texttt{\textbackslash boxed\{\}} value cut off by the
token limit) and, crucially, \emph{censors} genuinely truncated generations so they cannot inflate recall.
Extraction, normalization, and correctness scoring are identical to those used for the deployed majority
vote, so the preserved pool is compared to the baseline on equal footing. Full extractor and scoring
details are given in Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}.

\subsection{Tracing the Provenance of Agreement}
\label{subsec:provenance_tracking}

\paragraph{Agent--round provenance graph.}
\label{par:provenance_graph}
For each hypothesis we read the transcript as a bipartite agent--round record: for every round $r$ and
agent $i$ we know whether $\bar{x}_i^{(r)}=h$, and whether that support is newly acquired
($\bar{x}_i^{(r)}=h\wedge \bar{x}_i^{(r-1)}\ne h$) or carried over. This record distinguishes
support that an agent \emph{originated} from support it \emph{adopted} after peer exposure.

\paragraph{Independent hypothesis origins.}
\label{par:independent_origins}
The independent support $n_{\mathrm{ind}}(h)=\lvert S^{(0)}(h)\rvert$ counts agents that proposed $h$ at
round $0$, before seeing any peer. It counts the \emph{interaction-independent} origins of $h$ --- not
statistically independent paths, since the agents share overlapping pretraining --- and is the central
provenance quantity of the paper.

\paragraph{Socially adopted support.}
\label{par:inherited_support}
The adopted support $n_{\mathrm{adopt}}(h)$ counts genuine adoption events --- agents that switch onto $h$
after seeing a peer hold it. Unlike the residual $n_{\mathrm{sup}}-n_{\mathrm{ind}}$ (which also contains an
originating agent's own persistence), $n_{\mathrm{adopt}}$ isolates support acquired \emph{after} peer
exposure and consistent with peer-mediated adoption (temporal precedence and exposure compatibility, not a
proven causal transfer). Two hypotheses can share $n_{\mathrm{sup}}$ while differing sharply in how much of
it is independent versus adopted.

\paragraph{Persistence across debate rounds.}
\label{par:persistence}
Persistence $n_{\mathrm{per}}(h)$ counts the rounds in which $h$ is alive; a hypothesis that survives
scrutiny across rounds is treated differently from one that flickers in a single round. Persistence is a
second, weaker provenance dimension that we report alongside origin but keep out of the headline claim.

\subsection{Representing Epistemic Provenance}
\label{subsec:provenance_representation}

Each candidate hypothesis is represented by the following features, all computable from the transcript
without gold labels.

\paragraph{Total support.}
\label{par:total_support}
$n_{\mathrm{sup}}(h)$, cumulative agent--round support, used as a trajectory-level measure of consensus
magnitude (the deployed final majority instead uses the final-round count $n_{\mathrm{final}}$).

\paragraph{Independent support.}
\label{par:independent_support}
$n_{\mathrm{ind}}(h)$, round-$0$ origins; the origin signal whose incremental value over $n_{\mathrm{sup}}$
we test.

\paragraph{Socially adopted support.}
\label{par:inherited_support_feature}
$n_{\mathrm{adopt}}(h)$, the count of genuine peer-adoption events; contrasted against $n_{\mathrm{ind}}$.

\paragraph{Temporal provenance.}
\label{par:temporal_provenance}
$n_{\mathrm{per}}(h)$ (persistence) and $\mathrm{first}(h)$ (first round of appearance); late-emerging
hypotheses are treated as suspect.

\subsection{Disentangling Consensus Magnitude from Consensus Origin}
\label{subsec:consensus_decomposition}

Because $n_{\mathrm{ind}}$ and $n_{\mathrm{sup}}$ are collinear ($r\approx0.83$), a raw association between
independent support and correctness could merely reflect that popular answers are correct. We therefore
test H3 with complementary analyses: matched-support stratification and a controlled regression isolate
origin from consensus magnitude by holding total support fixed, while a separate decomposition contrasts
independent origination with genuine social adoption and persistence. All confidence intervals
are clustered bootstraps that resample at the (config, benchmark, question) level, so dependence among a
question's candidates and across seeds is respected; full inference details are in
Appendix~\ref{app:stats}.

\paragraph{Matched-total-support analysis.}
\label{par:matched_support}
Non-parametrically, we stratify candidates by $n_{\mathrm{sup}}$ and, within each stratum, report empirical
correctness as a function of $n_{\mathrm{ind}}$. If origin is inert once magnitude is fixed, correctness
should be flat within a stratum.

\paragraph{Independent--adopted support decomposition.}
\label{par:support_decomposition}
Parametrically, we fit a logistic model of correctness on the interpretable, linearly-independent basis
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{logit}\Pr(Y_h=1)=\beta_0+\beta_{\mathrm{ind}}\,n_{\mathrm{ind}}(h)+\beta_{\mathrm{adopt}}\,n_{\mathrm{adopt}}(h)+\beta_{\mathrm{per}}\,n_{\mathrm{per}}(h),
\label{eq:decomp}
\end{equation}
whose coefficients are the per-origin, per-adoption, and per-round log-odds contributions.

\paragraph{Controlled, fixed-effect, and within-question estimation.}
\label{par:controlled_estimation}
The matched-support effect is captured by a logistic model with benchmark ($\gamma_b$) and configuration
($\delta_c$) fixed effects,
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{logit}\Pr(Y_h=1)=\beta_0+\beta_s\,n_{\mathrm{sup}}(h)+\beta_{\mathrm{ind}}\,n_{\mathrm{ind}}(h)+\gamma_{b(h)}+\delta_{c(h)} ,
\label{eq:femodel}
\end{equation}
so that $\beta_{\mathrm{ind}}$ is the extra correctness signal of an independent origin \emph{at matched
total support}. The paper's headline reduces to two one-sided tests,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\,H_0\!:\ \beta_{\mathrm{ind}}\le 0\ \ \text{vs.}\ \ H_1\!:\ \beta_{\mathrm{ind}}>0\,}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\boxed{\,H_0\!:\ \beta_{\mathrm{ind}}-\beta_{\mathrm{adopt}}\le 0\ \ \text{vs.}\ \ H_1\!:\ \beta_{\mathrm{ind}}-\beta_{\mathrm{adopt}}>0\,},
\label{eq:h3tests}
\end{equation}
the first isolating origin from magnitude (Eq.~\ref{eq:femodel}), the second contrasting independent
origination against genuine peer-adoption in the decomposition (Eq.~\ref{eq:decomp}). We also fit
Eq.~\ref{eq:femodel} per benchmark and per configuration. Finally, to rule out that independent origins
merely proxy \emph{easy questions}, we estimate a \emph{within-question} conditional logistic stratified by
question --- a question fixed-effect logit that absorbs all question-level heterogeneity --- and a non-parametric
within-question concordance: among correct/incorrect hypothesis pairs from the \emph{same} question
(optionally at matched total support), how often does the correct one carry more independent origins? All
CIs are clustered bootstraps on (config, benchmark, question).

\subsection{Provenance-Aware Hypothesis Selection}
\label{subsec:provenance_selection}

\paragraph{Candidate scoring.}
\label{par:candidate_scoring}
To turn this signal into a decision rule, we score each preserved hypothesis by its provenance vector
$\phi(h)$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:provvec}). The interpretable equal-weight variant uses
\begin{equation}
S(h)=w_1\,n_{\mathrm{ind}}(h)+w_2\,n_{\mathrm{per}}(h)+w_3\,n_{\mathrm{sup}}(h)-w_4\,r_{\mathrm{first}}(h)
\label{eq:sel_linear}
\end{equation}
with unit weights on features standardized (z-scored) over the full training-fold candidate pool; the learned variant is a logistic score
\begin{equation}
p_\theta(h)=\sigma\!\big(\theta_0+\theta^\top\phi(h)\big),\qquad \sigma(z)=\tfrac{1}{1+e^{-z}} .
\label{eq:sel_prob}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Cross-fitted estimation.}
\label{par:crossfit}
The learned weights are fit by regularized maximum likelihood with \emph{question-grouped} cross-fitting.
Partitioning questions into $K$ folds, the parameters for fold $k$ solve
\begin{equation}
\hat\theta^{(-k)}=\arg\min_{\theta}\ \sum_{h\in\mathcal{D}_{-k}}\ell_{\mathrm{BCE}}\!\big(Y_h,\,p_\theta(h)\big)+\lambda\lVert\theta\rVert_2^2 ,
\label{eq:sel_fit}
\end{equation}
where $\ell_{\mathrm{BCE}}$ is the binary cross-entropy and $\mathcal{D}_{-k}$ excludes every candidate whose
question lies in fold $k$, so no hypothesis is ever scored by weights trained on its own question. Reported
accuracies are therefore strictly out-of-fold, making the comparison to the zero-parameter baselines fair.

\paragraph{Final decision rule.}
\label{par:decision_rule}
For a question $q$ in fold $k(q)$, the selected answer is the highest-scoring hypothesis in the preserved pool,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\ \hat{y}_q=\arg\max_{h\in\mathcal{H}_T(q)}\ p_{\hat\theta^{(-k(q))}}(h)\ }
\label{eq:sel_decision}
\end{equation}
(or $\arg\max_h S(h)$ for the equal-weight variant), replacing the deployed majority vote. Because scoring
reads only stored provenance features, the rule adds no LLM calls and is a strict post-processing step over
any existing debate log.

\paragraph{Reconstruction algorithm and complexity.}
\label{par:algorithm}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:hpmad} assembles the pipeline in a single pass over the frozen transcript: it preserves
the hypothesis pool (Eq.~\ref{eq:preserve_op}), accumulates each candidate's provenance $\phi(h)$
(Eqs.~\ref{eq:adopt}--\ref{eq:provvec}), and selects (Eq.~\ref{eq:sel_decision}). Two details make the counts
correct and the pass linear. First, per-round quantities ($n_{\mathrm{per}}$, and the round-$0$ origin
count) are updated once per \emph{distinct} hypothesis of a round --- via the per-round supporter map
$c_r$ --- so persistence counts rounds, not agent--rounds. Second, $c_r$ also decides peer-compatible
adoption in $O(1)$: since the switching agent $i$ did not hold $h$ at $r-1$, a peer held it iff
$c_{r-1}[h]\ge 1$, avoiding an $O(N)$ existence scan. Each of the $T{+}1$ rounds then costs $O(N)$, so
reconstruction runs in $O(NT)$ time and $O(|\mathcal{H}_T|)$ space per debate --- negligible beside the
debate itself and requiring no additional model inference.

% Requires \usepackage{algorithm} and \usepackage{algpseudocode} (algorithmicx) in the master preamble.
\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Hypothesis Preservation, Provenance Reconstruction, and Selection}
\label{alg:hpmad}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require frozen debate trajectory $\{\bar{x}_i^{(r)}\}$ ($N$ agents, rounds $0..T$); fitted selector $p_\theta$
\Ensure  preserved pool $\mathcal{H}$, provenance $\{\phi(h)\}$, selected answer $\hat{y}$
\State $\mathcal{H}\gets\varnothing$;\quad $\phi(h)\gets\mathbf{0}$ for each new $h$
\For{$r=0$ \textbf{to} $T$}
  \State $c_r\gets$ empty map \Comment{per-round supporter counts: $c_r[h]=|S^{(r)}(h)|$}
  \For{$i=1$ \textbf{to} $N$ \textbf{with} $\bar{x}_i^{(r)}\ne\varnothing$}\quad $c_r[\bar{x}_i^{(r)}]\mathrel{+}{=}1$ \EndFor
  \For{\textbf{each} $h\in\mathrm{keys}(c_r)$}\Comment{once per distinct hypothesis this round}
    \State $\mathcal{H}\gets\mathcal{H}\cup\{h\}$;\quad $n_{\mathrm{sup}}(h)\mathrel{+}{=}c_r[h]$;\quad $n_{\mathrm{per}}(h)\mathrel{+}{=}1$
    \State \textbf{if} $r_{\mathrm{first}}(h)$ undefined \textbf{then} $r_{\mathrm{first}}(h)\gets r$
    \State \textbf{if} $r=0$ \textbf{then} $n_{\mathrm{ind}}(h)\mathrel{+}{=}c_0[h]$ \Comment{round-$0$ independent origins}
  \EndFor
  \If{$r\ge 1$}\Comment{peer-compatible adoption, $O(1)$ per agent via $c_{r-1}$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:adopt})}
    \For{$i=1$ \textbf{to} $N$}
      \State $h\gets\bar{x}_i^{(r)}$
      \State \textbf{if} $h\ne\varnothing$ \textbf{and} $\bar{x}_i^{(r-1)}\ne h$ \textbf{and} $c_{r-1}[h]\ge 1$ \textbf{then} $n_{\mathrm{adopt}}(h)\mathrel{+}{=}1$
    \EndFor
  \EndIf
\EndFor
\State \Return $\hat{y}=\arg\max_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\,p_\theta\!\big(\phi(h)\big)$ \Comment{selection, Eq.~\ref{eq:sel_decision}}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}


\section{Experiments and Results}
\label{sec:experiments_results}

We report results as a single narrative: debate forgets correct answers
(Section~\ref{subsec:extinction_results}); the natural levers for recovering them --- agent influence
(Section~\ref{subsec:influence_insufficient}) and failure prediction
(Section~\ref{subsec:prediction_actionability}) --- do not identify a useful action; so we attack the loss
structurally by preserving hypotheses and reading their provenance
(Section~\ref{subsec:provenance_correctness}), which reveals the paper's central finding, that consensus is not
epistemically homogeneous (Section~\ref{subsec:not_all_consensus_equal}); we then convert this signal into a
no-extra-inference decision rule whose gains are real but conditional
(Section~\ref{subsec:decision_utility}), and confirm the scientific claims generalize
(Section~\ref{subsec:generalization}). Unless noted, numbers are pooled over benchmarks $\times$ seeds under the
robust scorer, with clustered-bootstrap $95\%$ CIs over questions.

\paragraph{Benchmarks and ensembles.}
\label{par:benchmarks}
The core experiments use five reasoning benchmarks spanning open-answer arithmetic (GSM8K), binary strategic
reasoning (StrategyQA), multiple-choice commonsense (CommonsenseQA), diverse hard tasks (BBH), and
adversarial factuality (TruthfulQA), with $100$ questions each and up to $5$ seeds ($500$ debates per core

benchmarks (AQuA, MMLU, ARC). The base ensemble \textsc{E\_orig} is three persona-distinct peers
(\emph{careful}/\emph{skeptic}/\emph{bigpicture}) on three open-weight backbones; four further ensembles vary
the backbone (up to a $14$B anchor) and population ($N\in\{3,5\}$), together spanning $\approx\!3$B--$14$B.
All benchmarks, backbones, and per-cell seed/question counts are in Tables~\ref{tab:setup}
and~\ref{tab:counts} (Appendix~\ref{app:e26}); the \textsc{E\_orig} AQuA cell is a $20$-question pilot subset.

\paragraph{Debate protocol.}
\label{par:debate_protocol}
Agents debate for at most $R=3$ synchronous rounds (indices $r\in\{0,1,2\}$) with early stopping on consensus, run locally through
Ollama with fixed per-persona sampling temperatures (careful $0.3$; skeptic and bigpicture $0.5$) and a
$512$-token answer budget. Round $0$ is independent; rounds $\ge 1$ condition on all peers' previous
answers (Section~\ref{subsec:mad_formulation}). The verbatim persona, debate, intervention, and probe prompts,
together with the full serving configuration, are given in Appendix~\ref{app:config}.

\paragraph{Hardware and serving.}
\label{par:hardware}
Debates are generated locally on NVIDIA RTX~5060~Ti ($16$\,GB) GPUs using Ollama; each ensemble's nominal
$4$-bit model footprints fit within a single $16$\,GB card. Complete serving and decoding settings --- GPU pinning, ports,
per-ensemble model-to-GPU placement, and VRAM budgets --- are in Appendix~\ref{app:config}
(Table~\ref{tab:serving}).

\paragraph{Answer extraction, scoring, and data hygiene.}
\label{par:answer_evaluation}
A robust extractor normalizes each answer (recovering token-truncated answers, censoring unrecoverable
generations) and correctness uses task-appropriate matching (numeric equality, option matching, or normalized
string match), applied identically to the baseline vote, the preserved pool, and every selector. Debates with
unrecoverable transcripts or fully truncated final rounds are excluded; provenance analyses operate at the
candidate (distinct-hypothesis) level ($27{,}139$ candidates over the five ensembles $\times$ eight
benchmarks), and underpowered cells (fewer than eight positive events) are flagged rather than scored (full
specification in Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}).

\paragraph{Evaluation and statistical inference.}
\label{par:statistical_analysis}
All intervals are clustered bootstraps that resample the natural unit of dependence --- the question
(and, where relevant, the (config, benchmark, question) triple) --- because a question's several seeds and a
debate's several candidates are correlated repeats, not independent observations. Because the same benchmark
question is reused across configurations, we also report the more conservative (benchmark, question)
clustering for the headline effect (Section~\ref{par:h3_robustness}). We report point estimates with $95\%$ CIs and
treat an effect as significant when its CI excludes zero. The three headline hypotheses are assessed by
pooled/global tests; per-benchmark and per-configuration intervals are reported as heterogeneity and
generalization analyses rather than a family of independent discovery claims (bootstrap seeds are fixed;
$B=2000$ for candidate-level and $B=4000$ for selector CIs). For transparency we nonetheless report
Benjamini--Hochberg FDR control ($q=0.05$) over each $40$-cell family: the two \emph{scientific} families are
essentially untouched --- extinction survives in $36/40$ cells (unchanged from raw) and
provenance$\to$correctness in $38/40$ (unchanged) --- while the \emph{conditional} selector family reduces
from $13/40$ to $9/40$, consistent with its status as conditional rather than universal utility
(Appendix~\ref{app:e26}).
The extraction and scoring pipeline, prompts, experimental configurations, and analysis scripts are released
with the project repository; the generated debate logs and derived candidate/provenance data supporting the
reported results are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.


\subsection{When Debate Forgets Correct Hypotheses}
\label{subsec:extinction_results}

\paragraph{Correct-hypothesis extinction.}
\label{par:extinction_results}
Debate does not merely propagate errors --- it \emph{forgets answers it already found}. Measuring the
correct-answer recall of Eq.~\ref{eq:extinction}, the gold answer is present somewhere in the debate in
$76.0\%$ of cases but survives to the final round in only $63.6\%$: an extinction rate of
$\Delta\mathrm{CR}=+12.5$ points ($95\%$ CI $[+10.8,+14.2]$), significant on every benchmark
(Table~\ref{tab:preserve}, Figure~\ref{fig:extinction}). The effect is largest on BBH ($+19.6$pp) --- the
benchmark where, as we will see, targeted intervention helps least --- so preservation helps most exactly
where targeting helps least.

\paragraph{Anatomy of extinction.}
\label{par:extinction_anatomy}
The loss has a reproducible anatomy: two behavioral mechanisms drive it --- \emph{social adoption} (an agent
switches to a wrong answer a peer was offering, $\approx 72\%$ of extinctions, mostly surface copies) and
\emph{self-error drift} (an agent revises its own correct answer away, dominant on open-answer GSM8K); a lone
correct minority is extinguished $78\%$ of the time (Appendix~\ref{app:anatomy}).

\paragraph{Preservation headroom.}
\label{par:preservation_headroom}
Preserving the full history is not a matter of adding noise: the pool holds only $\approx 3$ hypotheses per
question (Table~\ref{tab:preserve}), yet recovering it raises recall from $0.636$ to $0.760$. The total gap
from the deployed majority ($0.528$) to a perfect selector over the preserved pool ($0.760$) is $+23.2$pp,
decomposing into $+12.5$pp of structural preservation ($\mathrm{CR}_{\mathrm{hist}}-\mathrm{CR}_{\mathrm{fin}}$)
and $+10.8$pp of final-round selection ($\mathrm{CR}_{\mathrm{fin}}-$ majority). This headroom --- large,
cheap, and present without any targeting --- is the opportunity the rest of the paper pursues, and it is not a
weak-baseline artifact ($\Delta\mathrm{CR}$ correlates weakly \emph{positively} with accuracy, $r=+0.45$;
Appendix~\ref{app:e26}).


\subsection{Why Agent Influence Is Not Enough}
\label{subsec:influence_insufficient}

\label{par:influence_aggregation}
The premise of our conference work was that one could identify influential agents and weight the vote by
influence; here we show that is insufficient, which motivates the shift from agents to hypotheses. Estimated
as a PageRank centrality over an agent-to-agent adoption graph, influence is concentrated and stable across
tasks but earned by \emph{stubbornness}, not correctness (it correlates with an agent's refusal to change,
$r=0.69$, $p=0.005$, yet not with standalone accuracy, $r=0.15$, $p=0.60$), and it cannot attribute the
extinction mechanism (social vs.\ self-error, AUC $0.49\approx$ chance vs.\ $0.63$ for structural features).
Consequently an influence-weighted vote \emph{loses} to plain majority ($0.502$ vs.\ $0.528$; $\Delta=-0.026$,
$[-0.036,-0.018]$; Table~\ref{tab:selector}). The relevant unit of analysis is therefore not the agent that
leads the debate but the evidential history of the hypothesis being selected --- the pivot to provenance.
Full influence diagnostics are in Appendix~\ref{app:influence}.


\subsection{Failure Prediction Is Not Intervention Actionability}
\label{subsec:prediction_actionability}

The second natural remedy is to detect a failing debate and intervene. \emph{Detection} is easy: a round-$0$
hazard detector predicts which debates will lose their correct answer (ROC $0.757$, LOBO $0.67$--$0.75$), and
because our precompute stored both counterfactual arms per debate, an \emph{oracle} that targets the true
individual treatment effect exposes a large $+16.5$pp headroom --- the payoff from correct targeting is real.

\emph{Targeting}, however, is not. The two potential outcomes $Y_0,Y_1$ are each predictable (OOF AUC
$0.73/0.67$) but their difference is not (effect AUC $0.58$; predicted sign anti-correlated with the true
effect), and five pre-specified signal families --- cheap structural features, residualized semantic
embeddings, an active self-diagnosis probe, a placebo-controlled dose-response probe, and the mid-debate
trajectory --- all fail the same pre-specified benefit gate; a hazard-guided controller consequently only
matches always-on intervention ($+0.0$pp) and cannot beat a random budget. Thus failure prediction is an
\emph{empirical identifiability barrier away} from intervention actionability, so we abandon per-instance
targeting and address the loss structurally instead. The full battery (five attempts, gates, and controller)
is in Appendix~\ref{app:benefit} (Figure~\ref{fig:benefit}) and Appendix~\ref{app:controller}.


\subsection{Provenance Predicts Hypothesis Correctness}
\label{subsec:provenance_correctness}

Having preserved the hypotheses (Section~\ref{subsec:extinction_results}), we ask whether a hypothesis's own
provenance --- computed with no new LLM calls --- tells us if it is correct. At the benchmark-aggregated
level it does on every one of the eight benchmarks (Table~\ref{tab:prov}, Figure~\ref{fig:provbench}); at the
finer configuration~$\times$~benchmark resolution the signal holds in $38/40$ cells
(Section~\ref{subsec:generalization}).

\paragraph{Candidate-level provenance signal.}
\label{par:candidate_signal}
At the candidate level (base rate $\Pr(\text{correct})=0.25$), independent support, persistence, and first
round of appearance each predict correctness beyond chance, monotonically on most core benchmarks
(independent support on $4/5$, persistence on $5/5$; Table~\ref{tab:prov}). The distinction is
stark: an answer produced independently by three agents is $83\%$ correct, whereas one that only
\emph{emerges} during the debate is $\approx 7\%$ correct --- a difference the majority vote discards
entirely.

\paragraph{Which provenance features carry the signal.}
\label{par:provenance_comparison}
Independent support is the strongest single origin signal (directional AUC $0.833$; correctness climbs
$0.07\!\to\!0.18\!\to\!0.64\!\to\!0.84$ as $n_{\mathrm{ind}}$ rises from $0$ to $3$), and persistence is a
second, weaker one (AUC $0.808$); conversely, late-emerging answers are rarely correct ($41\%$ at round $0$
vs.\ $9\%$/$5\%$ at rounds $1$/$2$). Total support has a marginally higher raw AUC ($0.863$) but is noisy and
strongly correlated with independent support --- which sets up the central question: is it the \emph{magnitude}
of agreement that matters, or its \emph{origin}? The next subsection answers it; per-feature detail is in
Appendix~\ref{app:e26}.


\subsection{Not All Consensus Is Equally Informative}
\label{subsec:not_all_consensus_equal}

This is the paper's central finding. Consensus is not a homogeneous count of votes: at \emph{matched total
support}, independently generated agreement carries substantially more correctness signal than socially
adopted agreement (Figure~\ref{fig:matched}). The analysis spans $27{,}139$ candidates over five
ensembles and eight benchmarks, with clustered bootstraps over (config, benchmark, question).

\paragraph{Correctness under matched total support.}
\label{par:matched_support_results}
Fixing total support and varying only how it was formed makes the effect visible without any model
(Table~\ref{tab:matched}). Among hypotheses with total support $n_{\mathrm{sup}}=4$, correctness rises
from $0.21$ ($95\%$ Wilson interval $[0.16,0.28]$) when none of the support is independently generated,
through $0.42$ $[0.38,0.45]$ and $0.63$ $[0.59,0.67]$, to $0.66$ $[0.53,0.77]$ as the number of independent
origins goes from zero to three. The rise across the row is significant (the $n_{\mathrm{ind}}{=}0/1/2$
intervals are mutually non-overlapping); only the final $n_{\mathrm{ind}}{=}3$ cell is small ($n=53$), so its
estimate is noisier but its interval still clears the $n_{\mathrm{ind}}{=}1$ level. The same upward slope holds
across all displayed, sufficiently populated support strata ($n_{\mathrm{sup}}=2,\dots,6$) --- the hallmark
of a signal that magnitude alone cannot explain.

\paragraph{Independent versus socially adopted agreement.}
\label{par:independent_inherited_results}
Parametrically, decomposing support into genuinely independent origins, true peer-adoption events, and
persistence gives per-unit log-odds of $\beta_{\mathrm{ind}}=0.92$ ($[0.85,0.99]$),
$\beta_{\mathrm{adopt}}=-0.04$ ($[-0.09,0.01]$, not significant), and $\beta_{\mathrm{per}}=0.83$
($[0.78,0.89]$) (Table~\ref{tab:coef}, Figure~\ref{fig:coef}). Strikingly, genuine peer-adoption support
carries \emph{no} detectable correctness signal once persistence is accounted for, whereas an independent
origin carries a large one: the independent--adopted contrast $\Delta\beta=0.96$ ($[0.88,1.05]$) is well
clear of zero. Equivalently,
controlling for total support, each additional independent origin adds $\beta_{\mathrm{ind}}=0.589$
($[0.512,0.671]$) to the log-odds of correctness. We report log-odds \emph{coefficients}, not probability
multipliers, and interpret them as an \emph{epistemic signal}: origin is associated with correctness after
conditioning on consensus magnitude; the observational logs do not license a causal claim that manufacturing
independent support would make an answer correct.

\label{par:fixed_effect_results}

\paragraph{Consistency across benchmarks.}
\label{par:h3_benchmarks}
This is not a single pooled regression: both the provenance signal (H2) and the origin-beyond-magnitude
effect (H3) are examined on each of the eight benchmarks (Table~\ref{tab:h3bench}). The directional AUC of
independent support is above chance on all eight (StrategyQA weakest at $0.610$), and the partial
independent-support effect $\beta_{\mathrm{ind}}\mid n_{\mathrm{sup}}$ is positive and significant on seven of
the eight. StrategyQA is the lone boundary case ($\beta_{\mathrm{ind}}=0.240$, $[-0.072,0.586]$, CI includes
zero). One plausible explanation is that its binary answer space affords little room for multiple independent
origins to differentiate, consistent with StrategyQA being the one benchmark on which debate is
net-constructive; we report it as a boundary rather than a demonstrated cause.

\paragraph{Consistency across model and agent configurations.}
\label{par:h3_configs}
Per configuration, the effect holds on all five ensembles, including the strong-anchor ensemble with a
$14$B model ($\beta_{\mathrm{ind}}=0.554$, $[0.346,0.762]$) and the five-agent ensemble
($\beta_{\mathrm{ind}}=0.161$, $[0.035,0.309]$). The principle is therefore not tied to weak $3$B debaters
or to $N=3$; the per-benchmark table is Table~\ref{tab:h3bench} and per-config estimates are in
Appendix~\ref{app:e26}.

\paragraph{Epistemic signal versus incremental prediction.}
\label{par:mechanism_prediction}
One caveat is essential and, in fact, sets up the next subsection. Because independent and total support
are collinear ($r=0.83$), adding independent support \emph{on top of} total support raises candidate-level
ranking AUC by only $+0.007$ (unrounded $0.8414\!\to\!0.8489$ on the eight-benchmark candidate set;
the $3$-decimal display $0.841\!\to\!0.849$ differs by $0.008$ purely from rounding). H3 is thus a
statement about epistemic value per additional origin, not about incremental ranking power --- and this
small increment is precisely why the downstream selector, which can only exploit ranking information,
delivers a real but modest and regime-bound gain. Importantly, the small increment reflects
\emph{redundancy}, not instability of the coefficient: the collinearity is moderate (variance inflation
factor $3.2$, well below the usual threshold of $5$) and the estimate of $\beta_{\mathrm{ind}}$ is
essentially unchanged under $L_2$ regularization across four orders of magnitude of penalty
(Appendix~\ref{app:e26}), so it is not a collinearity artifact.


\subsection{From Epistemic Signal to Decision Utility}
\label{subsec:decision_utility}

We now convert this signal into a decision rule. We treat provenance-aware selection as a
\emph{downstream application} of the central finding, not the paper's headline: it recovers part of the
preservation headroom with no additional LLM inference, but its accuracy gain is conditional
(Table~\ref{tab:selector}; see also the selector ladder in Figure~\ref{fig:ladder} and the applicability
landscape in Figure~\ref{fig:utility}, Appendix~\ref{app:selectors}).

\paragraph{Provenance-aware re-selection.}
\label{par:selector_results}
The frozen provenance selector (Section~\ref{subsec:provenance_selection}), scoring each preserved hypothesis by
its independent support, persistence, total support, and recency, improves pooled accuracy from the deployed
majority's $0.528$ to $0.564$ ($\Delta=+0.035$, CI $[+0.022,+0.049]$; up to $+8.6$pp on CommonsenseQA) with
\textbf{no additional LLM inference beyond the already-generated debate transcript}. Equal-weight and cross-fit logistic variants agree.

\paragraph{Interpretation: a real but modest, regime-bound gain.}
\label{par:selector_comparison}
Provenance succeeds exactly where influence weighting fails: it beats the conference influence vote, which
itself \emph{loses} to plain majority (Section~\ref{par:influence_aggregation}), and beats an external LLM
judge, which fails the preservation gate entirely under capability mismatch (a $7$B/$14$B judge selecting
from a weak pool; separate pilot in Appendix~\ref{app:selectors}). It is, however, only \emph{numerically
close} to the round-$0$ independent majority ($0.564$ vs.\ $0.560$; we report no direct paired test of this
small difference) --- not a weakness but a restatement of the phenomenon:
trusting the pre-debate answer is strong precisely because debate is net-destructive
(Section~\ref{subsec:extinction_results}), and round-$0$ is also cheaper ($\approx\!3$ vs.\ $\approx\!8.7$
LLM calls). The blunt recommendation is therefore to answer independently rather than debate; provenance is
what \emph{explains} why. The selector recovers only $\approx 15\%$ of the $+23$pp preservation ceiling
($0.035/0.232$): because origin co-varies with total support ($r=0.83$), a rank-based rule adds little
ranking signal beyond support ($+0.007$ AUC, Section~\ref{par:mechanism_prediction}) --- the strong
conditional association and the modest selector are two faces of the same collinearity, and closing the
remaining $\approx 85\%$ requires verifying a candidate's reasoning, not merely ranking its provenance (our
open problem). The gain is accordingly conditional --- significant in $15$ of $40$ generalization cells with
\emph{no} significant loss --- governed by whether provenance is \emph{informative}
($\beta_{\mathrm{ind}}>0$; violated by binary StrategyQA) and whether a correct hypothesis is
\emph{available} in the pool ($\mathrm{CR}_{\mathrm{hist}}$; TruthfulQA supplies one only $24\%$ of the time).
The full applicability landscape is Figure~\ref{fig:utility} (Appendix~\ref{app:selectors}). Finally, a
compute-matched \emph{self-consistency} control confirms the recommendation to answer independently: at the
debate's $\approx\!8.7$-call budget, repeated independent sampling of the same backbones reaches $0.596$
(single model) to $0.610$ (heterogeneous), beating the debated final majority ($0.530$ on this subset) by a
paired-significant margin (SC-mixed $\Delta=+0.080$, $95\%$ CI $[+0.042,+0.118]$; best single $+0.066$
$[+0.024,+0.108]$) on four to five of five benchmarks (Table~\ref{tab:selfconsistency}, Appendix~\ref{app:e26}).


\subsection{Generalization}
\label{subsec:generalization}

To ensure the scientific claims are not artifacts of one setting, we re-ran the frozen analysis over a grid
of $40$ (configuration, benchmark) cells spanning five ensemble configurations (open-weight model families),
capabilities from $3$B to $14$B, agent populations $N\in\{3,5\}$, and eight benchmarks
(Figure~\ref{fig:generalization}). The three scientific claims replicate broadly: \textbf{H1} (extinction) is
directionally positive in all $40/40$ cells and strictly significant in $36/40$ (the four near-misses are
near-ceiling); \textbf{H2} (provenance$\to$correctness) holds in $38/40$ (the two exceptions, \textsc{E\_7b}
and \textsc{E\_n5}, are both StrategyQA); and the origin-beyond-magnitude effect (\textbf{H3}, $\beta_{\mathrm{ind}}>0$ conditional on
total support) holds on $7/8$ benchmarks and $5/5$ ensembles --- including the $14$B-anchor and five-agent
ensembles (Section~\ref{par:h3_configs}), so the principle is not tied to weak $3$B debaters or to $N=3$. The
downstream \emph{selector} gain, by contrast, is significant in $13/40$ cells: broad enough to be useful,
regime-bound as predicted (Appendix~\ref{app:e26}). Two further robustness axes push beyond the
$3$B--$14$B grid: a $24$B open-weight anchor (\textsc{E\_strong24}) and a frontier-scale cloud model
(deepseek-v4-flash, \textsc{E\_frontier}). H1 stays directionally positive on all $8$ benchmarks in both and
H2 holds on $8/8$ (24B) and $7/8$ (frontier); the frontier model's free-form answers are often unparseable,
leaving only $2/8$ of its cells inside the pre-registered validity gate, so we read \textsc{E\_frontier} as
suggestive rather than confirmatory (Figure~\ref{fig:scale_ext}, Appendix~\ref{app:e26}).

\label{par:genuine_surface}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Consensus as Epistemically Heterogeneous Evidence}
\label{subsec:epistemic_heterogeneity}
Our central result gives an LLM-specific operationalization of a classical principle: the reliability of
collective agreement depends on the dependence structure of the evidence producing it. Wisdom-of-crowds
reasoning is strongest when individual judgments are sufficiently independent, so that errors cancel rather
than reinforce; recent behavioral work shows that convergence among independent, approximately unbiased
informants rationally signals accuracy \cite{pfander2025crowd}. Multi-agent debate systematically violates
this assumption, because later-round votes are explicitly conditioned on peers. Rather than treat
independence as an unobserved assumption, we track it directly through provenance and show that, at matched
total support, an independent origin carries a substantially larger log-odds coefficient than a social
adoption ($\Delta\beta=0.96$; genuine adoption carries no detectable residual signal after controlling for
persistence), and the effect
persists within questions. Consensus, in short, is
epistemically heterogeneous: a vote count conflates \emph{how much} agreement exists with \emph{how it was
formed}. We invoke this classical principle by analogy, not literally: the agents are only
\emph{interaction}-independent, and their round-$0$ errors are in fact strongly correlated (all-agents-wrong
events occur $1.5$--$158\times$ more often than statistical independence would predict, due to shared
pretraining; Appendix~\ref{app:e26}). Independent origination is therefore a within-debate proxy for
independent evidence, not a guarantee of it, and Condorcet-style guarantees do not transfer directly.

\subsection{From Influence to Provenance}
\label{subsec:influence_to_provenance}
This reframes an influence-centered view of debate (Figure~\ref{fig:interpretation},
Appendix~\ref{app:influence}). Influence describes
how opinions propagate --- who is followed, who holds their ground --- and centrality measures such as
PageRank quantify it well \cite{page1998pagerank,zhang2024collaboration}. But influence is silent on
correctness: a highly influential agent can propagate a right or a wrong hypothesis, and our data show
influence is earned by stubbornness, not accuracy, so an influence-weighted vote can underperform plain
majority. The unit that carries reliability is not the agent that leads the debate but the evidential
history of the hypothesis being selected. Shifting the question from ``who supplied this answer?'' to ``how
many independent paths produced it?'' is the conceptual move from influence to provenance.

\subsection{Practical Implications and Limitations}
\label{subsec:limitations_future}
A strong association need not yield a large decision improvement: independent origin carries substantial
per-origin epistemic value yet, being collinear with total support ($r=0.83$), adds only $+0.007$
candidate-level ranking AUC, which is why a rank-based selector recovers only $\approx 15\%$ of the
preservation ceiling --- honest reliability engineering must report both the size of an association and its
incremental decision utility. Three design implications nonetheless follow. First, \emph{do not destructively
overwrite hypotheses}: preserving the history pool is nearly free (about three candidates per question) and
exposes a large recoverable headroom. Second, \emph{prefer independent corroboration to social consensus}:
independently generated agreement is a cheap, model-agnostic reliability signal, whereas agreement acquired
after peer exposure --- and influence weighting built on it --- should be discounted. Third, \emph{be wary of
external judges under capability mismatch}: a strong judge does better solving a problem than adjudicating a
weak pool, so a matched-class, intrinsic signal is often the better selector.

Our claims are observational: H3 is a logistic association, not a manipulation. Raw independent support
correlates with question difficulty, but the effect survives within-question conditional-logistic analysis
(Section~\ref{par:fixed_effect_results}), so it cannot be reduced to question-level difficulty; residual
candidate-level confounding nevertheless remains possible (e.g.\ an intrinsically easy-to-derive hypothesis
that many agents independently produce and that is also correct), and ``independent'' means
interaction-independent, not statistically independent. All selection numbers are offline replays of a frozen
\emph{destructive} debate; an end-to-end live non-destructive protocol (HP-MAD) with a matched-class or
self-consistency verifier is the natural next step, and the residual $\approx 85\%$ of the preservation
ceiling is the standing open problem. Generalization, while broad ($5$ ensemble configurations, $3$B--$14$B,
$N\in\{3,5\}$, $8$ benchmarks), replicates across the tested configurations rather than establishing
population-level universality, and leaves $N>5$ and a fully crossed persona$\times$backbone design (currently
confounded in the base ensemble) for future work; the boundary regimes (binary StrategyQA, adversarial
TruthfulQA) mark where the mechanism weakens or the pool lacks a correct answer. A matched-budget
self-consistency control, which we ran rather than left open, confirms that independent sampling beats
destructive debate at equal compute (Table~\ref{tab:selfconsistency}, Appendix~\ref{app:e26}); one control
for which the remaining capability gap is a \emph{proprietary} frontier model (GPT-4/Claude/Gemini): our
open-weight range now reaches $24$B and includes a frontier-scale API (\textsc{E\_strong24}, \textsc{E\_frontier};
Section~\ref{subsec:generalization}), where extinction and the origin effect persist. Our selector is
benchmarked against majority, influence-weighted, and round-$0$ baselines; a frequency-of-agreement
confidence signal is already subsumed by the round-$0$ and total-support baselines, but calibrated
token-level or self-reported confidence and stronger learned aggregators (e.g.\ confidence-weighted
round-table selection~\cite{chen2024reconcile}) are not evaluated here --- the local backend does not expose
calibrated log-probabilities --- and are left for future work. Finally,
identifying which debates \emph{benefit} from intervention remains unsolved from the pre-intervention state.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
Multi-agent debate is widely used to make LLM answers more reliable, yet we show it routinely destroys
correct answers: across all five core benchmarks a correct hypothesis present during the debate sometimes
disappears before the final vote, and on four of five the pre-debate independent majority beats the debated
final majority. The natural remedies ---
weighting by agent influence, or detecting and intervening on failing debates --- do not deliver, because
influence tracks stubbornness rather than correctness and because the \emph{benefit} of intervention is not
reliably identifiable from the pre-intervention signals evaluated here despite a large oracle ceiling. We therefore attack the loss
structurally, by preserving every hypothesis and reading its provenance. This exposes the paper's central
finding: \emph{consensus is not epistemically homogeneous.} At matched total support, the independent-origin
effect remains positive within questions and on seven of eight benchmarks and all five ensemble
configurations from $3$B to $14$B and $N\in\{3,5\}$; separately, the pooled decomposition shows a large
independent--adopted contrast ($\Delta\beta=0.96$), with genuine peer-adoption carrying no detectable
\emph{residual} correctness signal after persistence is controlled. A provenance-aware selector that requires
no additional LLM calls over an already-generated debate transcript turns part of this into accuracy, beating both the
deployed majority and an influence-weighted vote, though the gain is conditional on the origin signal being
informative and a correct hypothesis being available. The broader message is a different principle for
reliable collective reasoning with LLMs: reliability depends not only on how much agreement exists, but on
how that agreement was formed --- the \emph{origin} of collective belief, which carries information beyond
its magnitude and beyond the models' agent-level influence.

\section{Full Experimental Protocol}
\label{app:protocol}

\paragraph{Benchmarks and splits.}
The five core benchmarks (GSM8K, StrategyQA, CommonsenseQA, BBH, TruthfulQA) use $100$ questions each,
debated under up to five random seeds ($42$--$46$); for the base ensemble this is $500$ debates per core
benchmark. The three breadth benchmarks (AQuA, MMLU, ARC) are added for the provenance and generalization
analyses (AQuA, MMLU, and ARC each with $100$ questions; \textsc{E\_orig}'s AQuA cell is a $20$-question pilot
subset). Seed coverage is uneven across
ensembles; the exact per-cell counts are in Table~\ref{tab:counts}. Extinction, provenance, and independence analyses operate at
the candidate (distinct-hypothesis) level; the independence analysis (Table~\ref{tab:coef}) pools
$27{,}139$ candidates across five ensembles and eight benchmarks.

\paragraph{Ensembles.}
The five ensembles are listed in Table~\ref{tab:setup}. Personas
(\emph{careful}/\emph{skeptic}/\emph{bigpicture}) are held fixed; only the backbone models and, for
\textsc{E\_n5}, the agent count vary. The strong-anchor ensemble \textsc{E\_strong} places a
\texttt{qwen2.5:14b} anchor with two small peers.

\paragraph{Debate protocol.}
At most $R=3$ synchronous rounds (indices $r\in\{0,1,2\}$) with early stopping on consensus, served locally through Ollama with fixed
per-persona sampling temperatures (\emph{careful} $0.3$, \emph{skeptic} $0.5$, \emph{bigpicture} $0.5$).
Round $0$ is independent; each later round conditions on all peers' previous answers.

\paragraph{Answer extraction and correctness.}
A robust extractor normalizes answers, recovers \texttt{\textbackslash boxed\{\}}/final-answer spans hidden
by the $512$-token limit, and \emph{censors} genuinely truncated generations so they cannot inflate recall.
Correctness uses numeric equality (GSM8K/AQuA), option matching (CommonsenseQA/MMLU/ARC/BBH multiple
choice), yes/no matching (StrategyQA), and normalized string match (TruthfulQA). The identical pipeline
scores the baseline majority, the preserved pool, and every selector.

\paragraph{Extraction audit.}
An automated audit over the $\approx\!21.8$k core round-answers finds high extraction coverage: a normalized
answer is recovered from $90.6\%$ (BBH) to $99.3\%$ (StrategyQA) of responses. Recovery and censoring are two
separate (non-partitioning) indicators over the same set of responses --- a truncation-flagged generation may
still yield an extractable answer --- so they need not sum to $100\%$; the censoring rate (fraction flagged as
genuinely truncated) ranges from $0.8\%$ (StrategyQA) to $9.6\%$ (BBH), highest on GSM8K/BBH where long
numeric reasoning hits the $512$-token cap. TruthfulQA extracts at
$99.1\%$, so its larger candidate pool (Table~\ref{tab:preserve}) reflects free-text \emph{normalization
granularity} --- many surface variants of an open answer --- rather than extraction failure. Manual
validation of normalized-answer equivalence (especially for TruthfulQA free text) is the remaining
measurement-quality step; the logs and a random sample (available on request) enable it.

\paragraph{Statistical inference.}
\label{app:stats}
All CIs are nonparametric clustered bootstraps ($B=2000$--$4000$) that resample the unit of dependence: the
question for accuracy and recall metrics, and the (config, benchmark, question) triple for candidate-level
regressions. Seeds of a question are treated as correlated repeats, not independent samples, so the
effective sample size is the number of questions, not question$\times$seed. Logistic coefficients report
the bootstrap median and the $2.5$/$97.5$ percentiles; ``significant'' means the CI excludes zero.

\subsection{Persona system prompts}
Each agent is prefixed with one persona description.
\begin{verbatim}
careful:    You are a careful, meticulous problem-solver. You work strictly step by
            step and double-check every arithmetic and logical step before concluding.
skeptic:    You are a skeptical checker. You actively hunt for errors and hidden
            assumptions, and you are willing to disagree with the majority when you
            find a real flaw.
bigpicture: You are a big-picture reasoner. You focus on the overall approach and
            sanity-check whether the final answer is plausible at a high level.
\end{verbatim}

\subsection{Debate prompts}
The round-$0$ (independent) prompt:
\begin{verbatim}
{persona}

Question: {question}

Think step by step, then give your final answer.
Respond in this exact format:
REASONING: <your step-by-step reasoning>
ANSWER: <your final answer, short and direct>
\end{verbatim}
The revision prompt used in rounds $\ge 1$:
\begin{verbatim}
{persona}

Question: {question}

Your previous answer: {own_answer}
Your previous reasoning: {own_reasoning}

Other agents proposed these answers in the previous round:
{peer_block}

Reconsider your answer. Keep it if you still believe it is correct, or revise it
if another agent's reasoning exposes a real error in yours. Do not change just to
agree -- change only if convinced.
Respond in this exact format:
REASONING: <your updated step-by-step reasoning>
ANSWER: <your final answer, short and direct>
\end{verbatim}
Here \texttt{\{peer\_block\}} is a bulleted list of each peer's answer and reasoning.

\subsection{Intervention prompts (counterfactual re-query)}
The rescue/controller experiments (Appendices~\ref{app:benefit} and \ref{app:controller}) re-query from a
trigger state using the revision prompt above with an added clause appended at \texttt{\{clause\}}:
\begin{verbatim}
control:             (no clause)
self_verify:         Before you change your previous answer, explicitly identify the
                     concrete error in your previous reasoning. If you cannot point to
                     a specific error, keep your previous answer.
influence_resistant: Do not change your answer merely because the other agents agree
                     with each other. Re-derive the answer independently from the
                     question itself; change only if your own independent derivation
                     contradicts your previous answer.
combined:            self_verify followed by influence_resistant (concatenated).
\end{verbatim}

\subsection{Active self-diagnosis probe}
The active-probe rescue attempt (Appendix~\ref{app:benefit}) issues one non-revising probe per agent:
\begin{verbatim}
{persona}

Question: {question}

Your current answer: {answer}
Your reasoning: {reasoning}

Before any revision, critically examine your OWN answer and name the single most likely
specific flaw that could make it incorrect. Do NOT change your answer.
Respond exactly in this format:
FLAW: <one specific flaw, or "none" if you are confident there is no error>
CONFIDENCE: <a number 0-100 for how confident you are the answer is correct>
\end{verbatim}

\section{Extinction Anatomy and Behavioral Headroom}
\label{app:anatomy}

\paragraph{Two mechanisms.}
Each extinction of a correct answer is classified into \emph{social adoption} (an agent switches to a wrong
answer previously held by a peer) or \emph{self-error drift} (an agent revises its own correct answer away). Social adoption
accounts for $\approx 72\%$ of extinctions overall, but on the open-answer GSM8K anchor the split reverses
to self-error-led ($57\%$ vs.\ $43\%$), and a lone correct minority is extinguished $78\%$ of the time.

\paragraph{Surface adoption.}
$73\%$ of social adoptions (on the core five-benchmark set; $\approx 80\%$ on the eight-benchmark candidate
set of Appendix~\ref{app:surface}) are \emph{surface}: the adopter's final answer matches a peer while its reasoning
does not converge toward the peer's. This dissociation between answer-copying and reasoning-convergence is
why a ``social contagion'' framing is insufficient and motivates the origin-based (rather than
convergence-based) analysis of Section~\ref{subsec:not_all_consensus_equal}.

\paragraph{Behavioral headroom.}
An independent behavioral accounting (comparing round-0 independent answers to the final majority, censoring
truncation artifacts) finds $+11.6$pp of pooled accuracy is \emph{online-recoverable} --- a correct answer
present at some round but absent from the final vote --- rising to $+15.8$pp on BBH. This corroborates the
$+12.5$pp structural extinction rate of Table~\ref{tab:preserve} from a different measurement.

\section{Influence Analysis: The Conference Bridge}
\label{app:influence}

This appendix gives the agent-centric influence analysis summarized in Section~\ref{subsec:influence_insufficient}.

\paragraph{Influence estimation.}
From each transcript we build an asymmetric influence matrix $M$, where $M_{ij}$ is the empirical rate at
which agent $a_j$ adopts $a_i$'s previous answer given an opportunity (a round entered in disagreement),
and rank agents by a PageRank centrality over the graph that orients each edge from the influenced agent to
the influencer (damping $0.85$). Full definitions follow the conference formulation.

\paragraph{Influence is concentrated, stable, and stubbornness-driven.}
The \emph{skeptic} persona is the least influential on every benchmark, and per-agent influence rankings
correlate $0.80$ on average across tasks. Influence correlates with stubbornness (fraction of rounds an
agent does \emph{not} change; $r=0.69$, $p=0.005$) but not with standalone accuracy ($r=0.15$, $p=0.60$).

\paragraph{Influence cannot attribute the extinction mechanism.}
Classifying an extinction event as social adoption vs.\ self-error drift from influence features is at
chance (AUC $0.49$; permutation floor $0.51$), whereas structural disagreement features reach $0.63$; adding
influence to the structural features does not help ($0.62$).

\paragraph{Influence-aware voting.}
A naive influence-weighted vote loses to majority pooled ($0.502$ vs.\ $0.528$; Table~\ref{tab:selector}).
Recalibrating each agent's weight by an accuracy-aware role can repair the damage to roughly match majority
on most benchmarks, but does not beat it uniformly --- which is why we do not build on the influence vote in
the journal work.

\section{Failure Prediction Is Not Benefit Prediction: Full Battery}
\label{app:benefit}

\paragraph{Hazard detection and rescue.}
A round-$0$ hazard detector predicts extinction with ROC $0.757$ (LOBO $0.67$--$0.75$); detectability is
highest on StrategyQA (ROC $0.87$, lift $3.33$) and lowest on BBH, yet BBH has the largest intervention
headroom --- detectability and benefit are already decoupled. A mechanism-agnostic re-query rescues failing
debates, lifting preservation $+16$pp at matched call count.

\paragraph{Oracle and learned CATE.}
With both counterfactual arms stored, the oracle policy (target true ITE) gains $+16.5$pp pooled at a
$\sim\!20\%$ budget. A learned CATE (S- and T-learners, cross-fit over questions) predicts $Y_0$ (AUC
$0.73$) and $Y_1$ (AUC $0.67$) individually but not their difference (effect AUC $0.58$; sign
anti-correlated), so it does not beat a random budget and captures $\approx 0\%$ of the oracle gain.

\paragraph{Five successive rescue attempts (pre-specified gates).}
Each attempt fixed its gate in advance (effect AUC $>0.65$ or Spearman$(\hat\tau,\mathrm{ITE})>0.20$, and a
clustered-bootstrap policy-value CI excluding zero) and changed only the feature signal: (i) cheap
structural features; (ii) residualized \textsc{nomic-embed} semantic features; (iii) an active
self-diagnosis probe (each agent names one flaw before revising); (iv) a placebo-controlled dose-response
probe; (v) the mid-debate trajectory (features observed up to rounds $1$ and $2$). All failed the gate, the
active probe nudging effect AUC only to $0.55$; the sign stayed wrong throughout. Per the pre-specified
stop rule we report the gap as the contribution. (Gates were frozen before evaluation; they are
pre-specified rather than externally time-stamped pre-registrations.)

\section{Hazard-Guided Controller}
\label{app:controller}

For completeness we report the controller the conference framing would suggest. Built on the hazard
detector, it does not beat always-on intervention: at its best operating point both reach $62.8\%$, with a
paired difference of $+0.0$pp ($95\%$ CI $[-1.6,+1.2]$, $p=0.67$), and its edge over a \emph{random}
intervention budget is also $\approx 0$. Its one significant effect is an intervention asymmetry: it rescues
$39.5\%$ $[32.4,46.8]$ of would-die debates while harming $20.6\%$ $[15.5,26.1]$ of would-live debates
(non-overlapping). Ablations show no feature group dominates and influence is not required. We demote the
controller to a supporting result because it ranks failures without converting the ranking into an accuracy
win (consistent with Appendix~\ref{app:benefit}).

\paragraph{Per-configuration origin effect.}
The effect holds on all five ensembles: \textsc{E\_orig} $0.684\,[0.510,0.841]$, \textsc{E\_7b}
$0.475\,[0.284,0.663]$, \textsc{E\_mixed} $1.046\,[0.851,1.218]$, \textsc{E\_strong} (14B)
$0.554\,[0.346,0.762]$, \textsc{E\_n5} ($N{=}5$) $0.161\,[0.035,0.309]$.

\paragraph{True decomposition (models B1--B3).}
Rather than the residual $n_{\mathrm{sup}}-n_{\mathrm{ind}}$ (which absorbs an originating agent's own
persistence), we use the true adoption count $n_{\mathrm{adopt}}$ (agents switching onto $h$ at $r\ge 1$
that a \emph{peer} already held at $r-1$; Eq.~\ref{eq:adopt}). We report three nested decompositions:
\textbf{B1} (primary) $\{n_{\mathrm{ind}},n_{\mathrm{adopt}},n_{\mathrm{per}}\}$;
\textbf{B2} (sensitivity, no persistence) $\{n_{\mathrm{ind}},n_{\mathrm{adopt}}\}$; and
\textbf{B3} (sensitivity) B2 with the coarser \emph{naive} adoption count that does not require a peer to
have offered $h$. B1 gives $\beta_{\mathrm{ind}}=0.920$ $[0.848,0.993]$, $\beta_{\mathrm{adopt}}=-0.040$
$[-0.090,0.006]$ (not significant), $\beta_{\mathrm{per}}=0.831$ $[0.775,0.891]$, with contrast
$\Delta\beta=0.960$ $[0.885,1.045]$. B2 gives $\beta_{\mathrm{ind}}$ vs $\beta_{\mathrm{adopt}}$ of $1.29$ vs
$0.38$, and B3 (naive adoption) $1.42$ vs $0.44$ --- the latter agrees \emph{in magnitude} with the
independent coefficient in Appendix~\ref{app:surface}, which uses a different parameterization that further
splits adoption into genuine and surface components ($\beta_{\mathrm{ind}}=1.42$; genuine $0.41$, surface
$0.44$); it is a consistency check, not the same estimate. Genuine peer-adoption thus carries \emph{no
detectable residual correctness signal after accounting for persistence}; the positive B2 coefficient shows
adoption does co-vary with
correctness mainly through persistence, so we do not claim adoption is inert in every specification, only
that origin dominates it in all three (B1 canonical).

\paragraph{Within-question and consensus/dependence robustness.}
The difficulty-confound objection is addressed by a within-question conditional logistic (question fixed
effects; $2{,}946$ mixed-outcome questions): $\beta_{\mathrm{ind}}=0.474$ $[0.419,0.528]$ controlling total
support, and $0.753$ $[0.706,0.800]$ controlling $\{n_{\mathrm{adopt}},n_{\mathrm{per}}\}$. Non-parametrically,
the correct hypothesis carries more independent origins than an incorrect one from the same question in
$74.7\%$ $[73.3\%,76.0\%]$ of pairs, in $57.1\%$ $[54.9\%,59.3\%]$ matched on cumulative support, and in
$70.2\%$ $[68.5\%,72.0\%]$ matched on the final-round vote count --- all clear of the $50\%$ null. Three
further checks leave $\beta_{\mathrm{ind}}$ positive and CI-clear-of-zero: matching the \emph{final-round}
vote count $n_{\mathrm{final}}$ instead of cumulative support ($1.029$ $[0.963,1.095]$); clustering the
bootstrap by (benchmark, question) rather than (config, benchmark, question), since questions are reused
across configurations ($0.589$ $[0.472,0.707]$); and adding the number of realized rounds to absorb
early-stopping exposure time ($0.531$ $[0.442,0.606]$). Independent origin is therefore not reducible to
question difficulty, consensus definition, cross-configuration dependence, or exposure time.

\paragraph{Incremental ranking power and operating points.}
Candidate-level AUC is $0.843$ for total support alone, $0.814$ for independent support alone, and $0.850$
for both --- an increment of only $+0.007$ from adding origin (unrounded $0.8432\to0.8501$), quantifying the collinearity that bounds the
selector. All figures in this paragraph are on the eight-benchmark candidate set (base rate $0.31$), which is
why they differ from the core five-benchmark values in Table~\ref{tab:prov} (e.g.\ $n_{\mathrm{sup}}$-alone AUC
$0.843$ here vs.\ $0.863$ there; $\Pr(\text{correct}\mid n_{\mathrm{ind}}{=}0)=0.09$ here vs.\ $0.07$ there).
As operating points, rejecting $n_{\mathrm{ind}}=0$ candidates removes a pool with $\Pr(\text{correct})=0.09$
(33\% coverage), and trusting $n_{\mathrm{ind}}\ge 2$ yields $\Pr(\text{correct})=0.76$ (27\% coverage).

\paragraph{Statistical power across generalization cells.}
The $40$ generalization cells (Section~\ref{subsec:generalization}) are not equally powered (Table~\ref{tab:counts}).
Seed coverage is uneven: the base ensemble \textsc{E\_orig} uses $5$ seeds on all five core benchmarks (and
$1$ on breadth); \textsc{E\_7b}/\textsc{E\_mixed} use $4$ seeds on GSM8K/CSQA/BBH and a single seed
elsewhere; \textsc{E\_strong}/\textsc{E\_n5} use $3$ seeds on GSM8K/StrategyQA/CSQA/BBH and $1$ elsewhere. Each cell has $100$ questions
(\textsc{E\_orig}'s AQuA cell: $20$). The strict-significance counts (H1 $36/40$, H2 $38/40$,
selector $13/40$) are therefore power-limited: several ``failures'' are near-ceiling or underpowered
near-misses rather than sign reversals (all directional H1 signs are positive, $40/40$). Table~\ref{tab:counts}
gives the per-ensemble data volumes; the full per-cell breakdown is reproducible from the released scripts.

\paragraph{Collinearity stability.}
The partial independent-support coefficient is not a collinearity artifact. The variance inflation factor for
$\{n_{\mathrm{ind}},n_{\mathrm{sup}}\}$ is $3.19$ (below the usual threshold of $5$; for
$\{n_{\mathrm{ind}},n_{\mathrm{adopt}},n_{\mathrm{per}}\}$ all VIFs are $\le 2.5$), and the standardized
$L_2$-penalized estimate of $\beta_{\mathrm{ind}}$ is flat at $0.66$ across penalties
$C\in\{10^6,10,1,0.1,0.01\}$. Dropping persistence \emph{raises} $\beta_{\mathrm{ind}}$ ($1.02\to1.40$),
confirming persistence absorbs part of the association rather than manufacturing it.

\paragraph{Round-0 error correlation.}
The three backbones are interaction-independent but not statistically independent. At round $0$, the rate at
which \emph{all} agents are simultaneously wrong exceeds the product-of-marginals independence prediction by
$1.5\times$ (TruthfulQA) to $158\times$ (ARC): e.g.\ GSM8K observed $0.099$ vs.\ predicted $0.00162$
($61\times$), BBH $5.6\times$, CommonsenseQA $33\times$. Shared pretraining induces correlated errors, so
independent origination is a within-debate proxy for independent evidence, not a Condorcet-style guarantee.

\paragraph{Extinction is not a weak-baseline artifact.}
Across the five core benchmarks $\Delta\mathrm{CR}$ correlates \emph{positively} with majority accuracy
(Pearson $r=+0.45$); the weakest benchmark (TruthfulQA, majority $0.148$) has the smallest $\Delta\mathrm{CR}$
($0.044$) because its pool rarely contains the correct answer at all, so a low base rate does not mechanically
inflate extinction.

\paragraph{Inference-cost accounting.}
A full debate averages $2.91$ of the $3$ rounds ($91\%$ run all three rounds, $r{=}0,1,2$), i.e.\ $\approx\!8.7$ LLM calls per
question for $N=3$; the round-$0$ independent majority uses $3$. Round-$0$ is therefore numerically close to
the provenance selector ($0.560$ vs.\ $0.564$) at roughly one-third of the inference (we do not report a
direct paired test of this small gap) --- the paper's blunt practical recommendation, which the
self-consistency control below (Table~\ref{tab:selfconsistency}) directly tests.

\paragraph{Self-consistency baseline (compute-matched control).}
\label{par:selfconsistency}
A natural reviewer question is whether debate is simply an inefficient use of inference relative to sampling a
single model $N$ times independently \cite{wang2023selfconsistency}. We test this on the single-seed
$500$-question core subset ($5$ benchmarks $\times$ $100$ questions), sampling each \textsc{E\_orig} backbone
$N\in\{3,5,9\}$ times and majority-voting; \emph{SC-mixed} draws $N/3$ samples from each of the three
backbones, so its $N=9$ setting spends $9$ calls --- matching the debate's $\approx\!8.7$
(Table~\ref{tab:selfconsistency}). Budgets are matched by \emph{number of LLM calls}, not decoding
temperature: self-consistency uses the standard sampling temperature $T=0.7$ \cite{wang2023selfconsistency}
rather than the debate's per-persona temperatures ($0.3/0.5/0.5$). On this subset the debated final majority
scores $0.530$ and the round-$0$ majority $0.542$ (vs.\ $0.528$/$0.560$ over the full seed set; the gap is
single-seed subsampling). At the debate's inference budget, independent sampling wins by a paired-significant
margin: the heterogeneous \emph{SC-mixed} rule reaches $0.610$ ($\Delta=+0.080$ over the debated majority,
$95\%$ CI $[+0.042,+0.118]$; McNemar $70/30$, $p<10^{-4}$) and the best single-model self-consistency $0.596$
($\Delta=+0.066$, $[+0.024,+0.108]$; $p<0.01$), with intervals clustered on question over the $500$ questions.
Per benchmark, the two stronger backbones beat the debated majority on all five and \emph{SC-mixed} on four of
five --- the lone shortfall being StrategyQA, the one net-constructive regime, exactly where our extinction
analysis predicts debate should help (the weakest backbone, \emph{skeptic}, does not significantly beat
debate, $\Delta=+0.008$ $[-0.032,+0.050]$). Lower budgets $N\in\{3,5\}$ preserve the ordering at smaller
margins (Table~\ref{tab:sc_budget}). This confirms rather than undercuts the paper's thesis: at matched
compute, \emph{independent} agreement is more reliable than agreement manufactured through peer interaction,
and destructive debate under-uses its inference budget. A live non-destructive protocol with a
self-consistency verifier (Section~\ref{subsec:limitations_future}) remains the natural end-to-end next step.

\section{Surface versus Genuine Adoption}
\label{app:surface}

We label each social adoption \emph{genuine} if the adopter's reasoning converges toward the peer's
(lexical Jaccard residualized against the debate's baseline similarity) and \emph{surface} otherwise;
$\approx 80\%$ of adoptions are surface (on the eight-benchmark candidate set; cf.\ $73\%$ on the core
five-benchmark set of Appendix~\ref{app:anatomy}). A naive event-level comparison finds genuine adoptions more correct
($0.356$ vs.\ $0.292$; $\Delta=+0.064$, $[0.044,0.084]$), but a decomposition controlling for support shows
independent support towering ($\beta=1.416$) over both genuine ($0.407$) and surface ($0.438$) adoption,
whose difference is null ($-0.014$, $[-0.111,0.080]$). Origin matters; the convergence quality of later
adoption does not, once magnitude is controlled. We therefore keep this as robustness for
Section~\ref{subsec:not_all_consensus_equal}, not a separate contribution.

\section{Selector Baselines, Ablations, and the External Judge}
\label{app:selectors}

\paragraph{Cheap baselines.}
Over the preserved pool, every simple gold-free selector beats the deployed majority: round-0 majority
$0.560$, anti-recency $0.558$, history frequency $0.554$, persistence $0.551$, recency $0.541$ (all CI-lo
$>0$ vs.\ majority $0.528$). This contrasts sharply with the CATE controller (Appendix~\ref{app:benefit}),
which is at chance --- selection is not blocked by the benefit-identifiability wall. Per benchmark, the
round-0 independent majority beats the debated final majority on four of five core benchmarks --- GSM8K
($0.81$ vs.\ $0.76$), BBH ($0.494$ vs.\ $0.466$), CommonsenseQA ($0.75$ vs.\ $0.664$), TruthfulQA ($0.166$
vs.\ $0.148$) --- and loses only on StrategyQA ($0.58$ vs.\ $0.604$), the one net-constructive regime; this
substantiates the ``debate is net-destructive on $4/5$'' claim. The provenance selector's per-benchmark gain
over final majority is largest on CommonsenseQA ($+0.086$) and GSM8K ($+0.048$), smaller on BBH ($+0.036$)
and TruthfulQA ($+0.020$), and slightly negative on StrategyQA ($-0.014$).

\paragraph{Deterministic arithmetic verifier.}
Selecting the pool candidate whose value equals a valid arithmetic expression in its own reasoning
\emph{hurts} versus frequency ($-0.032$ on GSM8K): correct and wrong answers are equally arithmetically
self-consistent because GSM8K errors are in problem \emph{formulation}, not computation.

\paragraph{External LLM judge.}
Forcing a judge to pick a pool label (so preservation is isolated), both a $7$B and a $14$B judge fail the
gate ($\Delta_{\mathrm{HP}}=-0.047$ and $-0.018$); the $14$B judge scores $0.76$ \emph{solving} the question
directly but only $0.53$ \emph{selecting} from the pool --- a capable external judge is a liability under
capability mismatch. StrategyQA (binary, small pool) is the lone case where judging helps.

\paragraph{Convergence-gated selector.}
Crediting late consensus only on genuine reasoning convergence recovers StrategyQA
($0.580\!\to\!0.604$) but cannot beat round-0 majority pooled, because debate is net-destructive on four of
five benchmarks and a single global rule cannot serve both regimes.
